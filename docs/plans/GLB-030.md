## Objective

When the page opens, the whole Earth is in the globe stage with space
around it, at every screen size the layout knows, seen from above one
place that the project has chosen.

## Why

Since GLB-002 the page shows the Earth, with the view the engine opens
with by itself. That view is from one fixed distance, and the engine
holds its field of view, an angle, across the longer side of its
drawing surface. So the Earth takes the same share of the longer side
whatever the shape of the stage, and on a phone held upright, where
the stage is 390 pixels wide and 745 high, the Earth is wider than the
stage and is cut off at both sides. The tester's pictures of GLB-002
show it. A person who opens MotoSphere on a phone should see a globe,
not a part of one.

The picture of the Earth is checked from its pixels in GLB-003, and
the surface follows the size of the stage in GLB-028. Both need to
know what the globe shows when it opens, so this task comes before
them.

## What this task is, and what it is not

- **It is** one new module of arithmetic
  (`frontend/src/globe/homeView.ts`), which names the place and the
  share and works out the distance; the module that starts the engine
  setting the camera with it before the first picture; and the unit
  tests and browser tests of both.
- **It is not** what the view does when the stage changes its size
  after the globe has opened: a panel that opens, a window that
  changes, a phone that is turned. That is GLB-028. The tests of this
  task look at the page as it opens, at each size.
- **It is not** how a person moves the globe (GLB-005 and the tasks
  after it), nor a way back to this view (GLB-010).
- **It is not** a check of the whole picture (GLB-003). The browser
  tests of this task read a few places of it: enough to hold where the
  edge of the Earth is, which place is in the middle and that north is
  up.
- **It is not** a change to the sky, the engine's own mark or its way
  with the mouse: they are as the engine has them (GLB-005, GLB-012,
  GLB-015).

## Design in short

The new module, in full but for its comments:

    export const HOME = { longitude: -40, latitude: 30 } as const

    export const SHARE_OF_THE_SHORTER_SIDE = 0.8

    export function distanceOfTheWholeEarth(
      width: number,
      height: number,
      fieldOfView: number,
      share: number,
    ): number {
      const shorter = Math.min(width, height)
      const longer = Math.max(width, height)
      const shorterOverLonger = shorter > 0 ? shorter / longer : 1
      const halfTheShorterSide = Math.tan(fieldOfView / 2) * shorterOverLonger
      return 1 / Math.sin(Math.atan(share * halfTheShorterSide))
    }

The distance is in radii of the Earth, from its middle. Half of the
longer side is seen under half the field of view; half of the shorter
side under the angle whose tangent is smaller by the shape of the
surface; the Earth is to reach the share of that. And the edge of a
ball is seen under the angle whose sine is its radius over the
distance to its middle, which is where the last line comes from.

In the module that starts the engine, a function of its own, called
once, straight after the widget is made:

    function lookAtTheWholeEarth(scene: Scene): void {
      const { camera, canvas, ellipsoid } = scene
      const { frustum } = camera
      if (frustum instanceof PerspectiveFrustum && frustum.fov !== undefined) {
        const distance = distanceOfTheWholeEarth(
          canvas.clientWidth,
          canvas.clientHeight,
          frustum.fov,
          SHARE_OF_THE_SHORTER_SIDE,
        )
        camera.setView({
          destination: Cartesian3.fromDegrees(
            HOME.longitude,
            HOME.latitude,
            (distance - 1) * ellipsoid.maximumRadius,
          ),
        })
      }
    }

`Cartesian3` and `PerspectiveFrustum` are brought in from
`@cesium/core`, where the engine keeps them (see `ARCHITECTURE.md`, the
row of the globe); `Scene` is brought in as a type from
`@cesium/engine`.

## What the trial measured and settled

**The place and the share are the planner's choice**, and the person
whose project this is will be told of both with the result. The place,
30 degrees north and 40 degrees west, is the middle of the North
Atlantic: from above it Europe and North Africa are in view on one
side and the Americas on the other, and no continent is in the middle
to be taken for the subject. The share, four fifths of the shorter side,
leaves a tenth of that side as sky on either side of the Earth. Both
are one number each in `homeView.ts`, and changing one changes one
line and the unit test of that number.

**The engine's field of view** is 60 degrees when it starts
(`engine/Source/Scene/Camera.js`: `this.frustum.fov =
CesiumMath.toRadians(60.0)`), and the engine holds it across the
longer side of the surface (`core/Source/PerspectiveFrustum.js`: the
angle "will be used as the horizontal FOV if the width is greater than
the height, otherwise it will be the vertical FOV"). The module reads
the angle from the engine (`frustum.fov`) and does not write 60 down.
The engine could be set to a view without perspective, whose frustum
has no such angle; it does not start that way, and the type check
asks for the question, so the function leaves the engine's own view
where the question is answered with no. No test holds that branch: it
cannot be reached without changing the engine.

**The measured result.** In pictures of the page as it was built, at six
sizes of the window (the five of the tests and 1920 by 1080), the edge
of the Earth's disc was looked for in the eight directions of a
compass from the middle of the stage: going outward, the last point
before the picture stays dark. It was found between 0.987 and 1.026
of the radius aimed at, in all 48 readings; nearest to it to the east
and west and a little over it to the north, at every size. The
readings of the tests, a tenth of the radius inside and a tenth
outside, leave room on both sides of that. With the engine's own
view, before this task, the Earth took about six tenths of the longer
side of the stage at every size.

**Straight down and north up are what the engine does when it is told
a place and nothing else**: `camera.setView` with a destination alone
takes a heading of 0, a pitch of minus a quarter turn and no roll
(`Camera.js`: `orientation.heading ?? 0.0`,
`orientation.pitch ?? -CesiumMath.PI_OVER_TWO`,
`orientation.roll ?? 0.0`). The module names no orientation. That it
is so in the picture is held by the browser tests of the place: a
view with south up, with east up or turned by nine degrees fails them.

**The height.** The camera is put at a height above the ground, and
the arithmetic gives the distance to the middle of the Earth in radii:
so the height is that distance less one radius, times the engine's
largest radius of the Earth (`ellipsoid.maximumRadius`). The Earth is
taken for a ball there. It is flatter at the poles by a third of a
percent; a third of a percent of the radius the Earth has in the
picture is about half a pixel on a phone held upright (156 pixels) and
a pixel and a half on the largest stage measured (413 pixels).

**The size of the surface is there when the engine is started.** The
engine's style sheet has the drawing surface fill the element it is
in, from the moment the widget puts it into the page, so
`canvas.clientWidth` and `canvas.clientHeight` are those of the stage
on the line after the widget is made. The browser tests hold it at
five sizes: a planted defect that measures the window and not the
surface fails six of the seven.

**The sky moves, so the tests stop the clock.** Around the Earth the
engine draws the stars, the sun and the moon where they stand at the
time of the page. A test that reads the sky a tenth outside the edge
of the Earth could one day read the sun. Every test of the new file
gives the page one fixed time before it opens it
(`page.clock.setFixedTime`, noon on 21 June 2026 by the clock of
Greenwich), at which the readings were measured. The stars stay: a
star is a point or two wide, so the sky at a place is read at three
points two pixels apart and the darkest is taken.

**The time a test has.** A test of the globe has 90 seconds since
GLB-031, a task that was planned out of this trial. With the 30
seconds a test had before, in the planner's first run of the new file
three times over, with two other runs of tests on the machine, the
test of the tablet ran out of time once in 21, while it waited for a
picture of the page; and on GitHub's Windows machine the tests of the
globe that were there took up to four times as long with the whole
Earth in view as without. What was measured then is in `TESTING.md`,
"A test of the globe has 90 seconds". The new file sets no time of its
own. Its tests wait for the Earth to be drawn for at most sixty
seconds, as the tests of GLB-002 do since GLB-032, the second task
that was planned out of this trial: on GitHub's Windows machine the
globe said that the Earth is drawn up to fifteen seconds after the
page was opened, with this view, in 22 openings that were measured
(`TESTING.md`, "What a slow machine does to the tests of the
Earth"). A test of it that takes more than 30 seconds is still something
the planner wants to hear of. What the stage says to a person while
the first picture is on its way is GLB-004.

**The places the tests read were measured, not looked up.** The
picture of the Earth that comes with the engine (Natural Earth II) is
coarse, and where its coasts are in the picture was read from pictures
of the page, every two degrees along the line of 40 west and along
the line of 23 north. The numbers are in `TESTING.md`, "What was
measured for these numbers". The places the tests read lie four to six
degrees on either side of each coast.

**What the browser tests do not hold.** The numbers of the place and
of the share themselves: the tests hold that the picture is the view
from 30 north and 40 west to about seven degrees (a view seven degrees
off in any of the four directions fails both tests of the place;
smaller distances were not tried), and they take the share from the
module, so that a change of the share is one line. The two numbers are
held by the unit tests. And at one of the five sizes, the tablet held
upright, the sixteen readings of the edge pass for the engine's own
view too: a planted defect that never sets the view fails six of the
seven tests and not that one.

**The earlier tests.** The five browser tests of GLB-002
(`e2e/globe/earth.spec.ts`) read no place of the picture, and they and
every other browser test that was there pass unchanged. With the whole
Earth in view they take longer than before on GitHub's Windows
machine, which is what GLB-031 was for, and the engine draws the
complete picture more than once more there after its last piece has
arrived, which is what GLB-032 was for: before it, one of the five
failed with this view in both of the planner's runs there.
With both tasks, in the planner's trial on GitHub (run 46135317949),
the five passed at the first attempt on both machines and took 5.6,
5.4, 4.9, 1.1 and 14.9 seconds on the Linux machine and 2.4, 4.7, 13.3,
4.3 and 26.7 on the Windows machine.

+ * The view the globe opens with: the whole Earth, seen from above one place.
+ * This module is arithmetic only and knows nothing of the engine, so that

+/**
+ * The place the globe is above when it opens, in degrees: the middle of the
+ * North Atlantic. From there Europe and North Africa are in view on one
+ * side and the Americas on the other.
+ */
+export const HOME = { longitude: -40, latitude: 30 } as const
+
+/**
+ * The share of the shorter side of the drawing surface that the Earth takes
+ * when the globe opens. The rest is the space around it.
+ */
+export const SHARE_OF_THE_SHORTER_SIDE = 0.8
+
+/**
+ * How far from the middle of the Earth it has to be seen from, in radii of
+ * the Earth, to take the share of the shorter side of a surface of this
+ * width and height.
+ *
+ * The engine holds its field of view, an angle, across the longer side of
+ * the surface. Seen from one fixed distance the Earth would take the same
+ * share of the longer side whatever the shape of the surface, and on a
+ * surface much longer than it is wide, a phone held upright, it would be
+ * cut off at both sides.
+ *
+ * A surface that has no size yet is taken for a square one.
+ */
+export function distanceOfTheWholeEarth(
+  width: number,
+  height: number,
+  fieldOfView: number,
+  share: number,
+): number {
+  const shorter = Math.min(width, height)
+  const longer = Math.max(width, height)
+  const shorterOverLonger = shorter > 0 ? shorter / longer : 1
+  // Half of the shorter side, as the tangent of the angle it is seen under.
+  const halfTheShorterSide = Math.tan(fieldOfView / 2) * shorterOverLonger
+  // The edge of a ball is seen under the angle whose sine is its radius
+  // over the distance to its middle.
+  return 1 / Math.sin(Math.atan(share * halfTheShorterSide))
+}
