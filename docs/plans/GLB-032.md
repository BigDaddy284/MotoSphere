# GLB-032 – The tests of the Earth do not depend on the speed of the machine

**Phase:** 3 – Interactive 3D Earth
**Track:** B (see `TASKS.md`, "Two tracks")
**Depends on:** GLB-002, GLB-031 (both merged)
**Base branch:** `main`
**Read first:** `CLAUDE.md` §3 and §6, `docs/WORKFLOW.md`, `TASKS.md`
("Two tracks" and the entries of this task and of GLB-030),
`PROGRESS.md` (PLAN-061), `TESTING.md` ("The browser of the globe
tests" and "The Earth in the page"), `frontend/e2e/globe/earth.spec.ts`,
`frontend/src/globe/earth.ts`

## Objective

The five browser tests of the Earth pass on a machine that is slow,
and still fail for an engine that is wrong: they wait up to sixty
seconds for the Earth to be drawn, where they waited twenty, and the
one that looks at what the engine does afterwards holds that the
engine comes to rest, where it allowed one more picture.

## Why

Two of the five tests of `frontend/e2e/globe/earth.spec.ts` failed on
GitHub's Windows machine on 2026-11-28, each once, with nothing wrong
in the page.

1. **The Earth was not drawn within twenty seconds.** In run
   46131565305, of the pull request of DAT-067, which changes nothing
   of the globe, the test that stops and starts the engine failed at
   its first step, 27.6 seconds after it began: it had waited the
   twenty seconds of `DRAWN_WITHIN`, and the globe of the page had not
   said that the Earth is drawn. The other four tests of the file had
   taken 2.0, 8.0, 14.0 and 4.5 seconds in that run. The planner had
   the job run a second time, once, and it passed.
2. **Three pictures after the moment, where the test allows one.** In
   the planner's trial of GLB-030 (the view the globe opens with) on
   top of GLB-031, run 46130916825, the test that counts the pictures
   the engine draws after the globe has said that the Earth is drawn
   saw three, and in a second run of that view, 46133108330, two. The
   test allows one. The picture of the stage was the same pixel for
   pixel each time, and no request was under way.

Neither is a defect of the application. The first is a wait that
holds how fast a machine is. The second is a rule that is not true of
the engine: what it does after its last piece has arrived depends on
work it has under way in the background, and how much of that ends
after the moment depends on the machine and on the view.
`CLAUDE.md` §6: "Do not confuse an unavailable external service with
an application defect."

It is a task of its own and comes before GLB-030: it changes one test
file and nothing of the application, and GLB-030, which changes the
application, failed on the Windows machine in the last two runs of
its trial without it, after two runs before those had passed.

## What this task is, and what it is not

- **It is** a change to one test file,
  `frontend/e2e/globe/earth.spec.ts`: one number, and the last step
  of one test.
- **It is not** a change to the application: nothing under
  `frontend/src` changes, and a person who opens the page sees what
  they saw before.
- **It is not** a test taken away, and not a test that holds less
  than it was written for. The test of what the engine does after the
  moment was written to find an engine that draws all the time; it
  finds it still, and it finds every planted defect of GLB-002 that
  it found before (see "What the planner tried").
- **It is not** more time for a test: a test of the globe has 90
  seconds since GLB-031, and that stays.
- **It is not** a change to the settings of the browser tests, to the
  limit of the Windows job on GitHub (20 minutes, FND-057) or to the
  workflow.

## Design in short

**The wait.** `DRAWN_WITHIN` in `earth.spec.ts` is 60,000 where it
was 20,000: two thirds of the 90 seconds a test of the globe has. All
five tests wait with it (`untilTheEarthIsDrawn`).

**The rest.** The script the test gives the page before anything of
it runs (`NOTE_WHAT_THE_ENGINE_DOES`) already counts the pictures the
page's 3D pictures are told to draw. It now also notes when the last
of them was drawn, and gives, with the other notes, for how many
milliseconds none has been drawn (`still`). The test of what the
engine does after the moment had this as its last steps:

    // Long enough for an engine that had more to fetch or to draw to do so.
    await page.waitForTimeout(1_500)
    const later = notes(await page.evaluate(NOW))
    expect([...under]).toEqual([])
    expect(later.arrived).toEqual(said.arrived)
    expect((await stage.screenshot()).equals(picture)).toBe(true)
    // …
    expect(later.pictures - said.pictures).toBeLessThanOrEqual(1)

and has this now:

    await expect
      .poll(async () => notes(await page.evaluate(NOW)).still, {
        message: 'milliseconds since the engine last drew a picture',
        timeout: AT_REST_WITHIN,
      })
      .toBeGreaterThanOrEqual(AT_REST)
    const later = notes(await page.evaluate(NOW))
    expect([...under]).toEqual([])
    expect(later.arrived).toEqual(said.arrived)
    expect((await stage.screenshot()).equals(picture)).toBe(true)

with `AT_REST = 1_500` and `AT_REST_WITHIN = 30_000`. It waits until
the engine has drawn nothing for a second and a half, for at most
thirty seconds, and then holds what it held: no request under way, no
file arrived that had not at the moment, and the picture of the stage
the same pixel for pixel. The count of the pictures after the moment
is no longer held. An engine that draws all the time never lets a
second and a half pass without a picture, and fails where it failed
before, with another sentence.

## What the trial measured and settled

**What was measured, and where.** After the two failures the planner
put a measuring test into a trial of the view of GLB-030, which has
the whole Earth in it, and ran it on both of GitHub's machines (trial
pull request #235, run 46133108330, closed without merging; the
measuring test is not part of any task). It opened the page 22 times
on each machine, 14 times at 1280 by 720 and 8 times at 768 by 1024,
and the page noted every picture it drew and the moment its globe
said that the Earth is drawn.

| | Linux | Windows |
|---|---|---|
| The globe said that the Earth is drawn, after the page was opened | 0.6 to 0.7 s | 1.5 to 14.9 s |
| The test saw it | 0.9 s after the page was opened, every time | 1.7 to 15.1 s after |
| Pictures drawn after that moment | one, 22 of 22 times | one 8 times, two 9 times, three 4 times, four once |
| The last of them, after that moment | 0.01 to 0.02 s | 0.02 to 3.7 s |
| The longest time without a picture before the last one | 0.02 s | 3.7 s; more than 1.5 s in 6 of the 22 |

On the Windows machine the page went without a new frame for up to
4.8 seconds at a time, and in one opening the test saw the moment ten
seconds after the page had noted it.

**Why the engine draws after the moment.** It draws when something
has changed (`requestRenderMode`), and one of the things that count
as a change is a piece of work that ends in one of its background
workers: each one has it draw the picture again
(`engine/Source/Scene/Scene.js`, where the scene listens to
`TaskProcessor.taskCompletedEvent` and asks for a picture). With the
whole Earth in view the engine asks a worker, two or three times in
all but one of the 44 openings, for a structure with which it finds
the height of the ground under the camera
(`incrementallyBuildTerrainPicker`). On the
Windows machine those pieces of work ended after the moment in 14 of
the 22 openings, up to 2.5 seconds after it; on the Linux machine
they ended before it every time. With the view the engine opens with
by itself, which is what `main` has, the planner saw no work of that
kind on its own machine, in 28 openings with the processor slowed
four and eight times, and one picture after the moment each time. So
the test as it was passes on `main` and fails with the next task's
view, on a slow machine, some of the time.

**Sixty seconds for the Earth.** The longest wait that was measured is
15 seconds, and the one that failed was longer than 20. Sixty is two
thirds of the 90 a test has. The test that stops and starts the
engine, the longest of the five, took up to 35.1 seconds on the
Windows machine when it had the whole Earth in view (`TESTING.md`,
"A test of the globe has 90 seconds"), with its own wait among them.
A test that needs its sixty seconds in full and then the rest of its
steps as slowly as that would run out of the 90: that would be a
machine on which the Earth takes a minute, and the test says so.

**A second and a half without a picture, within thirty seconds.** A
second and a half is what the test waited before. An engine that
draws all the time had drawn its last picture 15 thousandths of a
second before the test looked for the last time, in the planner's
run; one that draws a picture every second never lets more than a
second pass. By this measure the engine as it is was at rest at most
2.9 seconds after the moment on the Windows machine and 1.5 seconds
after it on the Linux machine, in the 22 openings each. Thirty
seconds is ten times the first.

**Time, and not the frames of the page.** The planner first thought
of counting the animation frames of the page in which nothing is
drawn. The measurement spoke against it: on GitHub's Linux machine
the page got no animation frame at all for two to four seconds at a
time once the Earth was drawn, in at least 19 of the 22 openings.
Frames are no clock there.

**What the test no longer holds, and what it never held.** It no
longer holds how many pictures the engine draws after the moment: an
engine that drew ten more than it needs and then stopped would pass.
It does not hold that the engine draws nothing at all once it has
been still for a second and a half: in 6 of the 22 openings on the
Windows machine one more picture came later than that, the same
picture. And no test holds how soon the Earth is drawn: a globe that
says so after 25 seconds passes all five now, and failed all five
before, which the third and fourth prove-it steps show. How long a person waits for
the globe, and what they are told meanwhile, is GLB-004.

**The other four tests** change in nothing but the number they wait
with. The planner ran all 24 planted defects below against the 18
browser tests of the three files GLB-002 named, to see that no test
finds less than it did.

**GLB-030 comes after this.** Its trial is made anew on top of this
task, and its plan will say how its own tests wait for the Earth.
